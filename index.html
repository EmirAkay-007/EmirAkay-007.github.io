The autonomous dive

In the competition’s target task, the aircraft dives at a QR code on the ground and pulls out on its own. I designed and wrote the guidance and the QR detection. Scroll to fly the run, Below is the run, recorded from the onboard camera in ArduPilot SITL and Gazebo.

  1. APPROACHING

    Target coordinates arrive from the competition server. The run is refused below 80 m; the aircraft lines up on the target at about 100 m.

  2. DIVING

    Throttle to zero and nose down towards 45° in guided mode, with roll held to 15° so the camera stays on the QR code.

  3. RECOVERING

    The pull-out starts the moment the QR code is read, or when the predicted height loss would reach the 50 m floor, so the lowest point lands on the floor instead of below it.

  4. RESUMING

    Above 90 m the run ends. Every way out of it, including force-end, the watchdog and a lost link, goes through one exit that writes the normal flight envelope back.

On the real aircraft

The manoeuvre on the ARES aircraft, filmed from the ground during a test flight, diving from about 100 m.

Tuning the run shaped the guidance. An altitude sample can be half a second old by the time it is used, and ignoring that once let a run aimed at a 70 m floor bottom out at 61 m. The pull-out prediction now accounts for the age of every sample.

The brackets were added to the footage to mark the aircraft.

Finding the target

During the dive, the camera has to find a QR code in a frame that is moving, tilting and changing light. Each frame is decoded as it is first. If nothing is found, the detector works through contrast enhancement (CLAHE), illumination correction, sharpening, upscaling and motion deblur within a time budget for that frame, and the stage that worked is tried first on the next one.

On a 640×480 webcam stream it averaged 28.8 FPS over 750 frames, about 12 ms per detection, with zxing-cpp.

Ground control stations

Two generations of the ARES ground control station: the one I built in C# for the 2025 season, and the Python and Qt station the team flies in 2026.

ARE-İON

2025, C#, .NET 8, Windows Forms

  1. Link

    Serial MAVLink to a Pixhawk Cube Orange+. MAVLink v1 and v2 frames are decoded from the raw byte stream; the station sends its own heartbeat and reconnects on its own when the link drops.

  2. Telemetry

    Single-purpose fields refreshed five times a second. A value not refreshed for 3 seconds is shown as “no data”, so an old number is never read as a live one.

  3. Map

    A satellite map in an embedded WebView2 and Leaflet page, driven from C# five times a second: the aircraft turned to its heading, and areas typed in as coordinates drawn on top.

  4. Video

    The stream processed on board by a Jetson Orin NX, detection boxes included, received through a small TCP-to-HTTP relay.

Source and v1.0 release on GitHub

SihaInterface

2026, Python, PySide6 (Qt Widgets and QML), pymavlink

  1. Watch list

    The flight data the operator chose to see: speeds, attitude, GPS time and position, battery and altitude, over MAVLink on a worker thread separate from the interface.

  2. Camera

    The onboard video, where the QR detection runs, with two recorders: one of what the operator sees, and an evaluation video stamped with the competition server’s time.

  3. Map

    The own aircraft, the others reported by the server, no-fly zones, the geofence and the mission on one map.

  4. Dive controls

    Target coordinates taken from the server, and the start and force-end commands for the autonomous dive.

SihaInterface was created and mostly written by Muzaffer Kadir Belen; Yusuf İslam İnan wrote the route preplanner. I wrote the dive guidance, the QR detection and the flight-envelope handling behind them, and since August 2026 I maintain an independent continuation.
